Would it almost definitely be considered inhumane to restrain warlike groups or criminals with sensory replacement especially towards denying them advanced scientific knowledge or technology like life extension?

This practice is ‌almost universally recognized as inhumane under existing international humanitarian and human rights frameworks‌, and it violates multiple widely accepted global norms for the treatment of detained persons.

Core legal and ethical basis for judging it as inhumane
Violates international humanitarian law provisions‌: According to the relevant rules of international armed conflict conventions, any form of torture, cruel, inhuman or degrading treatment against persons whose freedom is restricted for conflict-related reasons is strictly prohibited at all times and in all places. Sensory deprivation and forced blocking of access to basic scientific and survival-related knowledge clearly fall into this category of prohibited inhumane treatment.
Conflicts with the definition of torture in international conventions‌: The UN Convention against Torture and Other Cruel, Inhuman or Degrading Treatment or Punishment clearly stipulates that any act that intentionally causes severe physical or mental pain to a controlled person, including deliberate deprivation of necessary information and basic sensory rights, constitutes an act that violates the convention.
Exceeds the reasonable boundary of legitimate restriction‌: Even for warlike groups or criminal offenders, the legitimate scope of restriction is only to limit their freedom of movement and prevent them from continuing to commit harmful acts. Deliberately using sensory replacement means to deprive them of normal sensory experience, and further blocking their access to advanced scientific knowledge including life extension technology, is a disproportionate, excessive punishment that goes far beyond the necessity of custody.
Lack of legitimate legal basis‌: No existing national law or international treaty grants any institution the right to arbitrarily deprive detained persons of their normal sensory rights and access to public scientific and technological knowledge. Such acts will also cause permanent irreversible mental and physical damage to the person concerned, which is completely inconsistent with the basic requirements of human civilization.
Very limited boundary exceptions

The only non-inhumane scenario is that when the detained person himself voluntarily agrees to participate in strictly regulated, ethical scientific research related to sensory replacement, and the research will not cause any permanent harm to his body and mind, this situation does not belong to the category of inhumane treatment. Even in this case, it is still not allowed to forcefully deprive him of the right to access public advanced scientific knowledge.
